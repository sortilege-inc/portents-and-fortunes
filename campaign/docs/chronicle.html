Chronicle

Session by session — the tale as it is told at the table.

The mountain has slept for twenty-four years. Nothing has happened. That is the thing that has happened.

Session One

The Road East

11 August 2026

Seiya Mori, daimyō of the province and of a vassal family sworn to the Agasha, has ordered a consolidation. Two or three villages are being given up entirely and their remaining people divided among the settlements that can still carry them. The mountains are hard, the terraces are failing, and a village past a certain thinness costs more in labour than it returns in grain. A samurai and a body of ashigaru have been sent to supervise the move.

The charge

Togashi Norikage is not one of them. His abbot, Togashi Oharu, has sent him east alongside the displaced households as an observer in a religious capacity — outside the daimyō's chain of command, responsible for moving no one. What he is to watch for was not specified.

The destination is White Flower Village. The village the people are leaving is not marked on any map he has seen.

What he watched

Asked afterward what he thought the temple wanted of him, he did not reach for the policy. He reached for the seating.

These are not people being scattered into empty country. They are being set down among strangers: households with no shared history, no inherited sense of who defers to whom, expected to become one village by arriving in the same place. The uprooting, he holds, is the smaller injury. There is a jostling for position coming, and no settled order to resolve it against.

So he spent the road on the people who would have to hold that order together — not the households at the bottom, but the family heads and the village elders, those with standing enough by office or by age to speak for the people walking behind them. If the elders and the appointed men arrived of one mind, there would at least be a basis on which to settle what came after.

The reading

He had no training for the forms this called for, and went at it through the Void: quiet, attentive, more listening than steering. He spent a Void point to seize the moment.

It held — the elders came to the same mind he had. The conversation cost him three strife, and while he was listening to them he registered something else on that road that had nothing to do with them.

The chill

There is a spiritual disturbance on that road. He has felt it, and he knows roughly where it lies. He does not know what it is.

What he holds

Most of the evening was given to questions rather than to the road — the first full accounting of what this monk actually believes. What follows is his own reasoning, reported as he gave it.

The mountain

He is twenty-four. The Wrath of the Kami erupts every winter, small and dependable, and has not done so in his lifetime; the Agasha who watch it read a quiet winter as an ill omen, and there have been twenty-four of them. A priest at his cradle said the mountain would not wake while he lived.

He is afraid the priest was right. Not that the waking would kill him — he does not hold his death and the eruption to be bound together, and expects to survive it unless he is standing near enough to burn — but that a life can be a clock, and that something is being counted toward. Twenty-four years of Agasha unease have done their work. He has come to half-believe them by osmosis, and thinks that others look at him and see the omen rather than the man.

If the mountain woke tomorrow, he would not reach for the priest's dishonesty. To hold that a prophecy binds the heavens is to grant humanity a power that does not belong to it. If the kami speak, what they say is received; if they change their minds, or if one of them takes a side against another, no human failure is involved, and there is nothing to be done with such a thing but accept it.

As a child the fear ran the other way. He was frightened it would erupt.

The Order, the wheel, and the dead

His parents urged him to the Order. He was sixteen when he made the climb to the High House of Light to pledge himself and take the ink said to be mixed with the blood of the kami.

The ise zumi remember their past lives. He does not. What he has instead are flashes of intuition that surprise him: a competence he has no right to, arriving and departing; a moment in which he acts so unlike himself that he looks back afterward and cannot account for it. His masters remember more. He puts his own blankness down to a cloudy mind, because to name himself a new soul would be presumptuous — and then follows the thought anyway. If the world were only the past rearranged, nothing new could occur, and things must sometimes be new. Could that be him. Why him.

He is named for an ancestor, a martial master of some brutality. If that man is in Yomi and watching, what Norikage feels from him is disapproval, and it is more comfortable to hope he has been reborn as someone who has never heard the name. The offerings continue out of familial obligation rather than love. He believes the man would be embarrassed of him. He will not say the man was wrong: the deeds could have been right, could have mattered, and that possibility disturbs him as much as the alternative would.

That peasants go to Meido and are reborn while samurai reach Yomi, he believes the way one believes the weather — passively, without having tested it. After all, in a rebirth, perhaps one becomes samurai. That the forgotten dwindle into clerks in Emma-O's courts he takes as fact.

The Fortunes

He seeks Enlightenment, which leaves the wheel entirely, and finds no contradiction in prayer. One prays to a Fortune on meeting an obstacle one cannot find a way through. Prayer is the side trail: better to walk around and rejoin the proper path than to halt at the foot of a wall one cannot climb.

The Dragon champion Kisshōten, Fortune of Fertility, as the eighth Great Fortune, and the Dragon cannot make children. He does not pretend this is faith. It comes out of need, and need first — with, he allows, some wisdom in taking relief where it can be found, so long as the taking is not itself dishonourable.

The Perfect Land Sect

The sect teaches that a chanted mantra carries the faithful past judgment to Shinsei's dwelling, without practice and without merit. He calls it subversive. Whatever good comes of it is outweighed by the apathy that follows: it empties accomplishment of its meaning and leaves no reason to do anything worth doing.

The sect also holds that the world has entered an Age of Declining Virtue and that the samurai are to blame. He does not argue that the claim is false. He argues that the sect is not positioned to make it — that if it is true it cannot be known until much later, and that saying it now only makes people readier to do nothing.

Told of a villager who chants it, he would report the presence and not the person; he is unwilling to name peasants going about their lives. He would rather argue with her, and not unkindly. What is the belief doing for the land, for the people she cares for, for the generation coming after? All will be well in the end, perhaps. But they live now.

Asked whether the doctrine might simply be correct — whether Enlightenment is available to a peasant without permission, and what that would leave his order for — he declines the road, and knows that he is declining it.

It can’t be true, therefore it isn’t. It can’t be true, therefore it is not worth considering as though it could be. I don’t say it is, because if it is true, I don’t want it. Togashi Norikage, on the possibility that the Perfect Land Sect is right

At the table. The first session ran mostly on system and character: a tour of the Atlas and the sheet, a walk through rings and approaches, strife and fatigue, Void points, opportunity spends, stances and conflict actions, damage and critical strikes — then the questioning above, and one real check to close on. Two earlier rolls (a Fitness test and a duel initiative) were teaching examples and are not part of the record. The player's reading of the posting — that the pressure worth playing is the mixing of households rather than their scattering — was adopted on the spot, and turned White Flower Village from the place being emptied into the place receiving.

Session Two

The Chant

13 August 2026

The path drops steeply toward White Flower Village, which sits high in absolute terms and low against the peaks around it. Cresting the rise, two things arrive at once: what is wrong with Genzō, and what is going to happen to the village below.

What is wrong with Genzō

The column has left its dead behind. That is the first half of it. The second half is that the knowledge these households carry does not travel: their expertise is not farming in general but these plots, worked by their families for generations — which slope holds water, which shelf frosts first, where the wall gives each spring. Set that knowledge down on a hillside three days east and it is worth very little.

Genzō is not inconvenienced. Every advantage that might have inclined a life toward success has been left on the other side of the mountains, and success here would have to be built again from nothing to exist at all. Norikage does more listening than speaking, on the read that this is a man who needs someone able to listen properly. He notes his own standing while he does it: the escort is conscripted military rather than samurai, which makes him the highest-ranking non-military person on the road.

The weight

Looking down at the village, a weight settles on him and he knows: within a year, White Flower Village will be uninhabited or uninhabitable.

It is not a warning he can act on. There is no cause attached to it, no mechanism, and nothing to point at. He lets it land, lets it settle into the status of a fact, keeps his face still, and goes on doing the thing he would have been doing anyway.

I can tell you how I feel about this, but this is not a direction. This is a fact. What I am effectively trying to figure out is, how do I give myself direction in light of this fact? The player, naming the problem in and out of character

The errand

Seiya Fusae draws him up to the head of the column and drops her voice — low, not a whisper. Norikage holds himself a half-step behind rather than level, ordering their positions to show deference; she catches it, and inclines her head very slightly.

Word was supposed to have gone ahead. She has had nothing back. Run down, make sure the elders are ready to receive the column, arrange somewhere for people to sleep while the longer arrangements are worked out. He need not return — wait there. Asked when the messenger was due back, she gives a wry smile: this has all been rather hurried. Hearing before arrival was aspirational, but she had hoped.

He does not charge the slope. He converts the fall into a hopping, lateral descent, spending gravity sideways so it never gathers into a stumble, and paces it so that as the ground levels toward the village he resolves back into a walk.

The introduction

He arrives to find two men talking — one older, in a straw hat; one very old, hunched over a stout walking stick, with a wakizashi at his belt that marks him as the only samurai in a village of peasants. Norikage sets his body so that the two of them are his audience without the gathering others being shut out, bows, gives his name as the monk Togashi Norikage, and states his business immediately, on the assumption no word has come.

He chooses to arrive on the orders of the daimyō Seiya Mori rather than in the name of his abbot — a distinction he weighed aloud before speaking it.

It is the first the village has heard of any of it. The man in the straw hat is Tōbei, who is not an elder but is married to one, and who leaves to fetch her. The old samurai is Kitsuki Sadao. His temper starts to rise — and then goes out of him. The default would be genuine outrage; something about the day has left him without the energy to reach it. It is also transparently not about Norikage, which Norikage marks down for later.

Sadao

His children are well: both in the southern Dragon lands, one in the Kitsuki provinces, one an Emerald Magistrate whose letters arrive from as far off as Crab lands. He was something like a regional administrator, and has lived and worked in this province his whole career. He does not care for travel.

The village is administered by four: Sadao himself, Ume, Heisuke the craftsman, and Nui, who walks pilgrims north to the shrine and back and advises them on what to make as an offering. By rank Sadao could administer it alone. There is no structural reason for him to share power. He has chosen to, he does not explain it, and Norikage notices.

The shrine north of the village is a Seidō to Fukurokujin, Fortune of Wisdom — once mortal, by legend, who mastered the way, learned to live on the breath of the universe rather than food and drink, and ascended. Martial artists venerate him. An offering is a thing made with one's own hands. Norikage asks the more useful question, which is why the road needs an escort at all: whether the danger has agency behind it. It does not. In places the wind will simply take a careless walker off the cliff. That is also why one old woman is escort enough.

Then he raises the political problem, humbly: the arriving villagers include no one of standing equivalent to Sadao. Rather than folding them wholesale beneath the existing structure, might there be room to bring them into structures Sadao already knows? Sadao is not a foolish man and has worked at the level of clan administration before. He plainly has feelings about all of it, and switches into administrator mode anyway. The column brings its own food stores, goats, stored rice — everything portable it owned — so nothing is short in the immediate term. That lets him get practical: for the first weeks, here is where the bodies can go, and we work outward from there.

The stables

Ume arrives with Tōbei, sour-faced, turns to Sadao — what's all this about? — and is strongly, vocally against it. Less than a minute later Heisuke joins, a big man in his late forties in a working apron, wiping his hands and tossing the cloth over his shoulder. The three bow and start catching each other up.

Norikage does not drive the conversation. He has delivered the situation; he will answer what is put to him. What he does instead is watch for the sticking points — chiefly whether Ume's opposition is personal or situational, and whether the hostility runs past what ordinary village administration can absorb.

Heisuke claps him on the shoulder and proposes clearing space in one of the stables. Norikage agrees, partly because the state of the stables will tell him exactly what welcome these people are walking into, and what kind of man makes that offer.

Session Three

Room Enough

18 August 2026

Two villages meet for the first time in the open ground before White Flower, and the question of whether the newcomers are welcome is settled by the person nobody was watching, for a reason nobody in authority understands.

The bow and the spit

Kitsuki Sadao straightens as the column comes in. It plainly costs him. He puts himself at the front of the village's people, making it clear without saying it that he is first among them — and Ume walks up and plants herself at his shoulder, chest out, though she does not reach it. For a commoner to stand as a samurai's equal in front of strangers is not done. He does not dispute it.

Fusae steps out ahead of her own people, stops the wagons with a gesture, bows slightly, and gives her name and her warrant: sent here by the daimyō. She had sent word ahead; she has had nothing back; are things prepared.

She approaches him with charm, and charm is the one thing that will not work on him. He bows. Then he spits at her feet.

Agasha don't come here. We've taken care of the place well enough ourselves. Kitsuki Sadao

The insult is real and it is also, in its way, a reading of the situation. By law the two of them are near enough equals and he is by decades her elder. The Agasha are a long way off. He is in the wrong for pushing back against a provincial daimyō's order, and he is not wrong that nobody has ever come up here to see how the village fares.

The terms

Sadao gathers himself and puts his price on the table. Samurai pass through here on the way north to the seidō and do not stop. He does not expect anyone to come back and look at what this merger has done to the fields, or to the houses, or to the people in them.

So: she will return in person by spring — a year from this early summer — and she will bring either new homes for these households or the resources and the hands to build them. That is his condition for the village receiving them at all.

Room enough

While the two people with rank in the field are deadlocked, Ume has been walking the line of newcomers, looking them over the way a careful buyer handles vegetables at market — not quite degrading, entirely assessing. She talks to Genzō. She talks to Kiyo a while longer. She ruffles the hair of a child of nine.

Then she walks up to Sadao and settles it.

They're gonna stay here. Ume, ending the argument

She keeps walking, waving the column on behind her — come on now, don't be shy, there's room enough for all of us — and the people begin filing past while the samurai and the officer are still at loggerheads. Heisuke had already given up on the talking some minutes earlier, made a remark about geese quacking at one another, and started shifting bundles off the wagons into a place they would keep.

What the lips said

Norikage does not understand what just happened, and goes looking. He watches the shape of it: Ume and Kiyo talking, a grouchy face breaking into a grouchy smile, Kiyo gesturing further down the line, hands being clasped, Kiyo turning to make introductions to Genzō and Rokurō. By the time Ume comes back along the line she has decided.

The oldest of the arriving elders is a Perfect Land devotee. That is why the village opened. Not policy, not the daimyō's order, not anything said by anyone with standing to say it: two believers recognised each other across a line of carts, and the question was closed.

The insight

The scene loosens into the ordinary business of arrival — bedding, a common pot, the ashigaru eating rice apart. Fusae and Sadao take a table and are polite to each other and say nothing worth saying. Norikage sits with what he now knows and asks for help with it.

He lays the quandary out honestly: two villages, both carrying a doctrine he was raised to hold mistaken and dangerous; that gathering them in one place is either containment or consolidation and he cannot tell which; and that he has done this in a settlement he is certain will not exist in a year. He wants these people to have lives worth living. He does not know how to speak to Fusae about any of it.

A rice ball

A girl of thirteen or fourteen presses food into his hands — a rice ball sitting in broth — and tells him to eat. He thanks her and asks her name, and gives his own. She asks whether he is a monk, and then whether he is from the seidō, the shrine north of the village.

He is not; he has never been. He says he hopes to go someday. She has already moved on down the line with the food.

The assumption is the interesting part. To a child of White Flower a monk is a man from up the mountain, and Norikage has been taken for one all afternoon without knowing it.

Where Sadao stands

One question left, and he spends it on the old magistrate: not on the sect directly, which would announce itself, but on a knotty problem in the ethics of summoning, offered as something that has bothered him for years.

Sadao takes the bait gladly. He has no formal training, but the village sits on a pilgrimage road and devout strangers have been passing through and drinking with him for decades; he holds his own better than most secular samurai could. Fusae drifts in and out of listening and contributes nothing.

What comes through is not stated, because it does not need to be. Which is reassurance and alarm in one motion. He and Fusae would recognise each other's ground. And he is the only authority in a village that has just doubled its Perfect Land population without noticing.

What he takes to bed

He asks, obliquely, whether all that theology has done anything to settle Fusae's mind about what she saw him singing. It has not come up. She is not treating him with any particular suspicion, and she is not raising it. He decides not to raise it either — not until there is privacy to raise it in — and there is none tonight.

He sleeps in the rafters of the barn, Compromised. In the morning three of the ashigaru have already gone.

His decision is made before he sleeps. He will go back to the abbot. Telling Fusae puts the report in the daimyō's hands and the hammer comes down on people whose only offence he has not even confirmed; not telling her, and being found out later, puts him under suspicion. But he does not report to her — and so the choice, for now, remains his to make rather than hers.

Session Four

An Accord

26 August 2026

Two weeks of mountain road lie between White Flower and the temple. What he likes about them is the presence of limits — borders everywhere, some of them emptiness, most of them as solid as the mountains. Nowhere does the world move too fast to take in. There is always something near enough to put a hand on.

What he does not notice

He knows the shapes of danger well enough to list them. Large animals, in country where a road is known but little walked. Banditry, in the geographies that lend themselves to being surprised — and he is no lucrative target, but he is an easy one. Weather and rockfall trouble him least: he knows to hug the wall against the wind, and to be careful where the stone looks ready to come down.

He is walking through exactly such ground, scree that has slid before and will slide again, when the knowledge fails to do him any good.

He is not being foolish. He is lost in his head, which on this road amounts to the same thing.

The thing on the path

He nearly walks into it. Eight feet of something greenish and solid, a rock held in one hand, and the two of them frighten each other equally. It says something to him. If the words are Rokugani they are the most gargled Rokugani he has ever heard, produced somewhere at the back of the throat.

A mountain troll. Nothing in his experience tells him what to do with the sight, and the incongruity alone is enough to hold him still.

The duel he chose

Offered a skirmish or a duel, he takes the duel — the shape reserved for two people who have agreed to face each other, applied to a thing that has agreed to nothing. The troll shoves him and he goes back several paces without falling.

I will be honest: I was not sure that it would work. I have never had the need to do that before. But desperation sometimes summons greatness out of us. Kaito Juri

May such a time not be called upon often in their lives, he says. He is glad to see them gone. And he is very much aware that he must give more time to training his body.

Theology is all well and good in a room full of courtiers and theologians. On the road, it is not a protection. Togashi Norikage

They were quite uncouth, he adds, and had no spiritual curiosity at all.

White Flower by nightfall

If they make good time for the rest of the day, the track reaches White Flower Village around nightfall. He wants to be there. The village needs to know about the dead on the mountain, because those burials have to be done properly — and one of the dead was its elder. It is also somewhere safe to spend a night, and he needs one. He was not maimed. He is very tired.
