The Wrath of the Kami

An active volcano at the heart of Kinenkan province. In ordinary years its winter eruption is as dependable as the season — dependable enough that the Agasha who study it read a winter without one as an ill omen rather than a mercy. It has given them that silence for twenty-four years, which is exactly as long as Togashi Norikage has been alive. The ground has lately begun to stir.

Kinenkan province · volcano · Agasha watch

Kyūden Togashi · The High House of Light

The ancestral seat of the Togashi family, set high in the northern Dragon peaks and reached by a climb that is difficult without being impossible — the ascent every ise zumi makes to pledge themselves and take the ink. Norikage made it at sixteen. Reports from monks in the field are meant to return here.

Northern Dragon mountains · seat of the Togashi

Reisui-ji · The Temple of Cold Water

Togashi Oharu’s house, and Norikage’s. It sits in a nook the mountain makes, and the nook does something to the wind: the approach is walked headlong into it to keep from being put down, and for the last stretch a traveller cannot hear whatever is beside them for the noise in their own ears. Then the air stops. The air itself is the wall, and behind it is not silence but the abrupt end of noise, which is a different thing and takes a moment to accept. Then the goats, and people talking somewhere.

Trees are scarce this high, so the temple is stone, as Dragon temples are — the Togashi hold stone to stand for the permanence of their order’s mission against the impermanence of everything else, and they build as stoutly and as defensibly as castles, self-sustaining because these are among the most northerly settlements in the Empire. Four buildings and a bathhouse behind a low wall above the gorge, and a hall. One building is kept for the sort of practice that is done sitting down: a low table, ink to be mixed, and two or three others already at it. There is a library, and what is in it is a Togashi library — among the ordinary texts, works written to be taken slowly and with a teacher, which do a stranger no good at all read cold.

Around thirty people are in and about the place at any hour, fewer than forty counting visitors, and everyone knows everyone. Outside the Dragon lands a house this size would be the Brotherhood of Shinsei’s to keep; here the Togashi keep it, and Brotherhood monks come and go.

South-east of Fuchi Village · a Togashi temple · Oharu’s house

White Flower Village

A village of the high Dragon passes, and the destination of Seiya Mori's consolidation order. Two or three villages of the province are being given up entirely and their people divided among the settlements that can still carry them; White Flower is one of those receiving. Norikage is walking east with one such group — an unnamed village's households, under the escort of a samurai and a body of ashigaru. What condition the place is in, and whether it can hold what is being sent to it, he does not yet know.

High passes · destination of the consolidation

Mountain Song Temple

A temple of the high country near the Agasha and Mirumoto boundary, south of Shiro Agasha, with the silent volcano somewhere on the horizon from most of its ground. Monks withdraw here for observance. As in most Dragon temples, the Togashi keep it and visiting Brotherhood monks are common in it, the two orders having long been comfortable in each other's houses.

Near Shiro Agasha · mountain temple

Seidō Fukurokujin

Built in the Sixth Century atop a high peak, and the largest shrine in the Empire devoted to Fukurokujin, Fortune of Wisdom and Mercy. It is also, by common account, the least resplendent shrine to any Great Fortune, and it earns that plainly: a torii stands near the foot of the mountain so that every visitor makes the climb, and what waits at the top is a flat stone where gifts are laid. There is no hall, no roof, and nothing to shelter in. The road to the foot is reasonably traversable, which makes this the most readily accessible holding in the Togashi lands; the climb after it is not, and there is nowhere at the end of it to stay.

By the popular account the Fortune was once a mortal who mastered the Way, learned to subsist on the breath of the universe rather than on food and drink, and ascended to his divine post. He is shown as an elderly bearded man, stout and short, with a high cone-shaped forehead, leaning on a long staff with a book of lore, attended by a turtle, a crane, or a black deer. Martial artists venerate him as the wisdom sought through practice. His holy symbol is the needle and his most devoted worshippers are tailors. It is said he knows how to revive the dead, and neither uses nor shares that knowledge for fear of its misuse.

Tradition asks an offering made with the petitioner’s own hands rather than bought. The gifts run from the crude to the sublime and none are taken away, so the stone and the ground around it have become a museum nobody built: probably the most complete cross-section of artistic achievement in the Empire, arranged by nothing but the order in which people arrived, and left out in the weather to take what the weather gives. What it sorts its visitors into, over the years, is those who had something to make and those who did not.

A Kitsuki’s gempukku includes the pilgrimage here and three days spent in contemplation, and many return through their lives to ask the Fortune’s help in untangling a hard matter.

White Flower Village sits below it, and the pilgrim road up is walked rather than climbed until the last of it. The danger on that road is weather and footing and nothing with intent — in places the wind will take a careless walker off the cliff — so one villager who knows the way is escort enough. White Flower has one: Nui, who takes pilgrims up and back, and coaches them on what to make before they go.

Six people were lately killed on the stone and left arranged in a circle among the offerings. Togashi Norikage and Kaito Juri came up the pilgrim road two days later and found them: she cleansed the plateau and called the kami back to it, and he carried the six below the torii and raised cairns over them, there being no shovel on a mountain of rock. One of the dead was Nui, who had walked that road all her life.

The Refuge of the Three Sisters

A moon cultist temple, and one of the few anywhere that is openly what it is — a rare shrine to Lord Moon in the Dragon peaks, standing near the Wrath of the Kami and reached only by a dangerous ascent. People go to it to have questions answered. It is said to appear different to every visitor, and that three women — maiden, matron, and crone, the phases of the moon — will answer any question put to them, always in riddles. It is also said that misfortune befalls those who dare to ask. Both halves of that reputation are repeated most confidently by people who have not made the climb.
