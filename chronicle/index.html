The Record of Play

Chronicle

Session by session — the tale as it is told at the table.

The mountain has slept for twenty-four years. Nothing has happened. That is the thing that has happened.

Session One

The Road East

11 August 2026

Seiya Mori, daimyō of the province and of a vassal family sworn to the Agasha, has ordered a consolidation. Two or three villages are being given up entirely and their remaining people divided among the settlements that can still carry them. The mountains are hard, the terraces are failing, and a village past a certain thinness costs more in labour than it returns in grain. A samurai and a body of ashigaru have been sent to supervise the move.

The charge

Togashi Norikage is not one of them. His abbot, Togashi Oharu, has sent him east alongside the displaced households as an observer in a religious capacity — outside the daimyō's chain of command, responsible for moving no one. What he is to watch for was not specified.

The destination is White Flower Village. The village the people are leaving is not marked on any map he has seen.

What he watched

Asked afterward what he thought the temple wanted of him, he did not reach for the policy. He reached for the seating.

These are not people being scattered into empty country. They are being set down among strangers: households with no shared history, no inherited sense of who defers to whom, expected to become one village by arriving in the same place. The uprooting, he holds, is the smaller injury. There is a jostling for position coming, and no settled order to resolve it against.

So he spent the road on the people who would have to hold that order together — not the households at the bottom, but the family heads and the village elders, those with standing enough by office or by age to speak for the people walking behind them. If the elders and the appointed men arrived of one mind, there would at least be a basis on which to settle what came after.

The reading

He had no training for the forms this called for, and went at it through the Void: quiet, attentive, more listening than steering. He spent a Void point to seize the moment.

It held — the elders came to the same mind he had. The conversation cost him three strife, and while he was listening to them he registered something else on that road that had nothing to do with them.

The chill

There is a spiritual disturbance on that road. He has felt it, and he knows roughly where it lies. He does not know what it is.

What he holds

Most of the evening was given to questions rather than to the road — the first full accounting of what this monk actually believes. What follows is his own reasoning, reported as he gave it.

The mountain

He is twenty-four. The Wrath of the Kami erupts every winter, small and dependable, and has not done so in his lifetime; the Agasha who watch it read a quiet winter as an ill omen, and there have been twenty-four of them. A priest at his cradle said the mountain would not wake while he lived.

He is afraid the priest was right. Not that the waking would kill him — he does not hold his death and the eruption to be bound together, and expects to survive it unless he is standing near enough to burn — but that a life can be a clock, and that something is being counted toward. Twenty-four years of Agasha unease have done their work. He has come to half-believe them by osmosis, and thinks that others look at him and see the omen rather than the man.

If the mountain woke tomorrow, he would not reach for the priest's dishonesty. To hold that a prophecy binds the heavens is to grant humanity a power that does not belong to it. If the kami speak, what they say is received; if they change their minds, or if one of them takes a side against another, no human failure is involved, and there is nothing to be done with such a thing but accept it.

As a child the fear ran the other way. He was frightened it would erupt.

The Order, the wheel, and the dead

His parents urged him to the Order. He was sixteen when he made the climb to the High House of Light to pledge himself and take the ink said to be mixed with the blood of the kami.

The ise zumi remember their past lives. He does not. What he has instead are flashes of intuition that surprise him: a competence he has no right to, arriving and departing; a moment in which he acts so unlike himself that he looks back afterward and cannot account for it. His masters remember more. He puts his own blankness down to a cloudy mind, because to name himself a new soul would be presumptuous — and then follows the thought anyway. If the world were only the past rearranged, nothing new could occur, and things must sometimes be new. Could that be him. Why him.

He is named for an ancestor, a martial master of some brutality. If that man is in Yomi and watching, what Norikage feels from him is disapproval, and it is more comfortable to hope he has been reborn as someone who has never heard the name. The offerings continue out of familial obligation rather than love. He believes the man would be embarrassed of him. He will not say the man was wrong: the deeds could have been right, could have mattered, and that possibility disturbs him as much as the alternative would.

That peasants go to Meido and are reborn while samurai reach Yomi, he believes the way one believes the weather — passively, without having tested it. After all, in a rebirth, perhaps one becomes samurai. That the forgotten dwindle into clerks in Emma-O's courts he takes as fact.

The Fortunes

He seeks Enlightenment, which leaves the wheel entirely, and finds no contradiction in prayer. One prays to a Fortune on meeting an obstacle one cannot find a way through. Prayer is the side trail: better to walk around and rejoin the proper path than to halt at the foot of a wall one cannot climb.

The Dragon champion Kisshōten, Fortune of Fertility, as the eighth Great Fortune, and the Dragon cannot make children. He does not pretend this is faith. It comes out of need, and need first — with, he allows, some wisdom in taking relief where it can be found, so long as the taking is not itself dishonourable.

The Perfect Land Sect

The sect teaches that a chanted mantra carries the faithful past judgment to Shinsei's dwelling, without practice and without merit. He calls it subversive. Whatever good comes of it is outweighed by the apathy that follows: it empties accomplishment of its meaning and leaves no reason to do anything worth doing.

The sect also holds that the world has entered an Age of Declining Virtue and that the samurai are to blame. He does not argue that the claim is false. He argues that the sect is not positioned to make it — that if it is true it cannot be known until much later, and that saying it now only makes people readier to do nothing.

Told of a villager who chants it, he would report the presence and not the person; he is unwilling to name peasants going about their lives. He would rather argue with her, and not unkindly. What is the belief doing for the land, for the people she cares for, for the generation coming after? All will be well in the end, perhaps. But they live now.

Asked whether the doctrine might simply be correct — whether Enlightenment is available to a peasant without permission, and what that would leave his order for — he declines the road, and knows that he is declining it.

It can’t be true, therefore it isn’t. It can’t be true, therefore it is not worth considering as though it could be. I don’t say it is, because if it is true, I don’t want it. Togashi Norikage, on the possibility that the Perfect Land Sect is right

At the table. The first session ran mostly on system and character: a tour of the Atlas and the sheet, a walk through rings and approaches, strife and fatigue, Void points, opportunity spends, stances and conflict actions, damage and critical strikes — then the questioning above, and one real check to close on. Two earlier rolls (a Fitness test and a duel initiative) were teaching examples and are not part of the record. The player's reading of the posting — that the pressure worth playing is the mixing of households rather than their scattering — was adopted on the spot, and turned White Flower Village from the place being emptied into the place receiving.

Session Two opens on the disturbance, and on the first sight of White Flower Village from above.
